LIFETIME FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

I. The Economy In Which We Live And Our Daily Bread

1. The Industrial Economy 

!$P 1. As a family or as a single person, we are members of a national economy and society making our contributions to its performance, and being rewarded for our efforts according to the value the economy and society determine for them. The reward we get for our efforts as income is what we use to pay for our current needs and to provide for the future. As a family or as a single person, we live our lives as independent people, but the twists and turns the economy and society as a whole take over time work their influence on what fate has in store for us. The better we understand how the economy works and its interaction with our society, the better we will be at managing for today and planning for tomorrow. So let us look into the nature and motivation of the modern economy. 

!$P 2. In a society we live by what we produce. And in a world made up of national economies, we trade some of what we produce with other countries for what they produce. What we produce and what we get in trade with other countries is based on the demand we place on ourselves for privately produced goods and services as well as those goods and services we insist be produced by government. Individually, we exert a demand for some of those goods and services according to our unique preferences and the income we have to satisfy those preferences. 

!$P 3. A modern economy is based on money as a means for exchanging goods and services. There is a circular flow of activity in the production of goods and services in which workers of all kinds are compensated individually with money for what they do, representing their share of those goods and services available in the marketplaces of the economy. For any one individual, however, that income may be not only be in the form of wages and salary, but also in the form of dividends from ownership in producing firms, interest from loans made to others, payments by government, rent from property, and gifts from others. 

!$P 4. Wages or salary as a share of goods and services depend on the value of a person's labor in producing those goods and services; what that value might be is determined not only by the availability of labor skills, but by the demand of the public at large for the goods and services produced by those skills. The compensation of a highly talented professional athlete, a construction worker, a medical specialist, an office worker, a movie actor, and a computer programmer depends on their availability and the value the public at large puts on what they produce. For most walks of life, education plays an important role in the income paid to individuals for the work they do. So much of what the public wants today requires skills developed only through higher education. 

!$P 5. The need for those skills is traceable to successive scientific discoveries which have been exploited through investment in the means to produce goods and services that enhance our standard of living. The greater skills required to produce more valuable output are reflected in higher real wages and salaries which are transformed into a demand for the very goods and services they are instrumental in producing. So we can see if an economy is to enjoy increases in its standard of living there must be investment in new products and better ways to produce all products. Ther must also be increases in wages and salaries reflective of greater output, and a transformation of that income into demand for the greater output. 

!$P 6. Investment in new plant, machinery and new production techniques takes place where there is a promise of profit from the marketing of goods and services produced by the investment. Investment in productive capital in an advanced economy is made by entrepreneurs large and small. A large industrial firm like General Motors invests in improved means to produce autos it already produces, while one or a few individuals on a very small scale may create an entirely new product such as as personal computer by the investment of their time and meager resources. In both instances, the motivation is expectation of profit. Both ventures may prove to be successful. In the one, a large company replaces existing plant and equipment with more efficient capital to produce the same product. In the other, daring entrepreneurs start an entirely new industry to produce a product new to the consuming public, but a product they believe will be in demand for the efficiences and conveniences it opens up for its users. 

!$P 7. Whatever form it takes, investment in capital in a modern economy expands the capabilities of labor while men and women, to keep up with advancing vintages of capital must successively respond to the requirements for new and improved skills technology imposes on them. 

!$P 8. Capital and labor are the key ingredients for producing goods and services. But natural resources are an equally important ingredient. An industrial economy could not exist in the absence of access to natural resources. Some economies such as the United States are favored by providence and good fortune with an abundance of productive agricultural land, minerals, fossil fuels, water and a relatively benign climate. Other economies such as Japan may not have all they need as industrial powers within their borders but have access to them through trade with other countries. 

!$P 9. The world today is divided into industrial economies with advanced standards of living and economies in various stages of development with much lower standards of living. Why there are such great disparities in industrial capabilities is a question that has no ready answer. But one can be sure that the persistent buildup of capital, continuing improvement in labor skills and assured access in one way or another to natural resources are indispensable to industrial capabilities. The underpinning of it all seems to be an enlightened and educated population from which have come the scientific discoveries on which industrial processes are based; a population which has fomented the spirit of entrepreneurship; and a population which has provided not only the labor skills compatible with technological applications of scientific discoveries, but the demand for the goods and services producible by industrialization. The industrial age in which we live began in Great Britain some 250 years ago. By that time Great Britain and Western Europe were already technologically far ahead of the rest of the world. Along with what would become the United States and later Japan, their further development rested on the characteristics of their populations as described above. 

!$P 10. Were it somehow possible for an economy to maintain demand for output in keeping with its supply as improvements in goods and services were made, the economy could grow steadily at rates governed by variations in discoveries and the investment necessary to take advantage of them. As it is, demand for output and the supply of output only intermittently and temporarily coincide with one another. At any one time each is either rising or falling for its own reasons. Changes in output and supply that differ from one another underlie the undulations of the business cycle, a phenomenon of the industrial economy in which producers of output and consumers of output are generally  free to follow their own objectives. Generally, producers seek to maximize profits from marketing the goods and services they produce. Consumers seek to maximize their level of satisfaction from consuming goods and services arrayed before them in the marketplaces. By and large, the array of goods and services available to consumers depends in part on the capacity to produce them that prior investment makes possible. What prior investment has been made was motivated, as observed above, by expectations of profit in satisfying anticipated demand for products that investment has made possible. Whatever form investment takes, whether to produce a new product, reduce the cost of producing an existing product or expand the capacity to produce more of the same product, there is always some uncertainty for the individual producer about how much of what is produced will be absorbed by demand. At a time of high output and employment, demand for what has been produced is strong, and producers tend to think demand will continue and even grow stronger and that production can expand to satisfy that rising demand with little impact on average cost to produce. Their optimism may be borne out for a while but soomer or later they will be disappointed for several reasons. In an industrial economy, any one good or service may be produced by a number of firms in competition with one another. Each of  the firms typically will sense the same opportunity, and together in producing more in time almost certainly will produce more than the market, that is, demand, is willing to absorb at current prices, dampening their enthusiasm for maintaining output at current levels. At the same time, the increase in employment made by all producers in raising production will be made typically only at higher wages and salaries, further reducing the difference between revenue and cost. 

!$P Then, too, consumers buy many of their goods and services at irregular intervals. To be sure, a family eats its meals quite reularly and tends to use electricity, water and gas at predictable rates, but buys a new auto, buys new furniture or takes a vacation only infrequently, and buys a new home even less frequently. The comsumption patterns of the typical family in general exhibit irregular ups and downs that by themselves make it all but impossible for producing firms to anticipate. 

There is yet another aspect of the industrial economy which bears on both demand and supply in the marketplaces for goods and services. In all industrialized countries, governments routinely intervene in economic activity; the industrial economy can be said to be a mixed enterprise economy made up of private enterprise and government. In the United States, the federal government through its control of the money supply, and through its spending and taxes and borrowing, affects what consumers buy and what firms produce. Changes in the amount of money in circulation and interest rates on borrowed money represent for the most part changes in monetary policy which is the responsibility of the Federal Reserve System, an independent agency of the federal government. The Board of Governors of the System, appointed by the President, invokes changes in the money supply and interst rates by pumping money into the banking system or by siphoning it out. The FRS does that by buying or selling outstanding U.S. Treasury securities in transactions with the public at large. When the FRS buys Treasuries, it pays for what it buys with a check drawn on itself, in effect turning the Treasury security into money. That is, the amount of the FRS check in itself is an addition to the money supply. But there very well might be a further expansion of the money supply. The U.S. banking system operates on a fractional reserve basis; that is, a bank need set aside in cash only some small fraction of deposits it takes in, and can loan the rest of it to firms and individuals, increasing the money supply by the amount loaned out. An original infusion of money by the FRS into the banking system can thus result in a multiple expansion of itself, as it is passed along from bank to bank in this fashion. 

The FRS can bring about a reduction in the money supply by not replacing some U.S. Treasury securities it holds as they mature instead of buying them and adding to what it already has. When the FRS does not replace a U.S. Treasury security in this fashion, it gets a Treasury check drawn on some bank in payment for the maturing security. The bank in making good on the check with cash, to conform with legal requirements must replensih its cash by restricting the total of its outstanding loans. The reduction in bank credit amounts to a reduction in the money supply, and the need for the one bank to reduce its loans is passed along to the rest of the banks so that there is a multiple reduction in the money supply. 

Changes in the money supply cause changes in interest rates. When banks are flush with money as excess reserves, being profit oriented firms they are eager to make loans and offer the general public their excess cash at lower interest rates. On the other hand, when banks' legal reserves are dwindling due to FRS restrictive transactions in U.S. Treasury securities, the interest rates they charge for loans are bound to rise since demand for loans exceeds their willingness to make loans at current interest rates. 

Availability of credit is a key element in the demand by consumers for goods and services, and in the investment in plant, machinery, and inventory by producing firms to satisfy consumer demand. Thus, changes in monetary policy cause changes in national output and employment, or as we shall see momentarily changes in prices. When credit is readily available to consumers, they typically buy more. In buying more, they encourage firms to produce more, and in producing more, firms add to their employed work force. When credit is hard to come by, consumers typically buy less. In buying less, they influence firms not to produce more but to produce less and to reduce their work force. The question arises why there should be a need for changes in monetary policy. A practical answer is that an economy based on free enterprise is not a finely tuned watch which keeps time drawing its energy to do so precisely and continuously from a spring or a battery without sudden surges or reversals. Quite unlike a watch, the economy is subject to undulations resulting from the independent and uncoordinated decisions of producers and consumers. Those undulations are increases and decreases in output and employment. The objective of policy is to maintain monetary conditions conducive to full employment at stable prices. Ideally, through continuous adjustments in monetary conditions, it could evoke and perpetuate full employment output at stable prices. But the economy from time to time may be trying to produce more than it actually can, causing the general price level to push to higher and higher ground, or it may fall into a slump in which there are many men and women in the work force without jobs. So monetary policy seeks to moderate those ups and downs of the economy by causing changes in monetary conditions, making credit easy when hard times seem to be approaching, and slowing down its use in times when the economy seems to be reaching beyond its means. That is, the most it can achieve; but that is not to say that implemented policy can bring about moderated undulations of the economy with any degree of regularity. Some slumps last only a very short while; others go on for a long time. Good times with high employment and stable prices may be fleeting or long lasting. Policy makers can hope to make good times last, and to dispense with hard times quickly, but various influences on the economy are beyond their control. From scientific discoveries, changes in population, wars, outcome of capital investment, and changes in what government provides, the economy may be controlled by monetary policy only slightly or not at all. 

Monetary policy, too, contends with the impact that government has on the economy by its own spending and the redistribution of income it makes for some of what the economy produces, the taxes it collects, and money it borrows from time to time to finance its spending. What the federal government does in this respect has a profound effect on what the economy produces and how much it produces. The federal government is no bit player in the national marketplace. 

Its spending for goods and services in 1997 was $  billion in an economy producing a total of $  billion. Various categories of its spending were a significant proportion of total demand for those goods and services. In the case of certain classes of defense hardware, federal government spending absorbed all that was produced. Clearly, government demand for goods and services affects not only what is produced, but how much is produced and the capital investment that private enterprise makes to produce those goods and services. 

All of what the government does in this area is referred to as fiscal policy. Before the Great Depression of the 1930s, federal government procurement and transfer payments in peacetime were small. Since the early 1930s, however, peacetime budgeted outlays of the federal government have expanded for two reasons. Initially, the Roosevelt administration invoked increases in federal government outlays as a compensatory initiative, believing that greater spending by the federal government would set off a general expansion of output to pull the economy out of its desperate slump. Until quite recently, successive administrations have continued the use of compensatory fiscal policy in attempts to maintain demand for output at high employment levels. Coincidentally, various pieces of federal legislations added to the social, economic and regulatory responsibilities of government, emphasizing social welfare progams, and elevating outlays to permanently higher levels. 

Whether or not discretionary increases or decreases in outlays to compensate for high unemployment or inflationary pressures have been effective is not a settled question. Essentially, compensatory fiscal policy of the federal government entails spending more than the tax money it takes in during hard times, and spending less than it takes in during boom times, pumping up overall demand for output in hard times and trimming it back during boom times. 

When the federal government spends more than it takes in, it runs a deficit, and must borrow money from the public at large to make up the difference. Each annual deficit adds to the accumulated public debt of the United States. Should the government spend less than it takes in, it has the opportunity to reduce its total debt. Up until the last few years, since World War II the administrations that have followed one another had made use of compensatory policy not by running deficits or surpluses according to economic conditions, but by adjusting the size of the annual deficit, and during the Reagan administration, annual deficits actually increased while output and employment were expanding. When the federal government borrows money to finance some of its spending, it adds to all those seeking borrowed money, causes by itself interest rates to rise and seemingly eliminates some private investment that otherwise might have taken place. It is reasonable to think that government borrowing in bad times would have that effect on interest rates, if any, to a much lesser degree than it would when times are good. 

Proponents of compensatory policy believe the absence of a repetition of the Great Depression over the last 70 years is proof enough that it works. Critics, on the other hand, insist that changes in spending are perverse in the way they affect economic conditions, especially because of lags as they make their way through the economy, and wind up much of the time having effects opposite to those intended. 

In the last few years, federal government spending has come under pressure as a public obligation for social purposes as well as a compensatory device. In these prosperous times, public support for social welfare programs in general is at low ebb, and in the face of rising output and employment in spite of dramatic reductions in annual federal budgetary deficits, there is little of the enthusiasm for compensatory fiscal policy there once was. The general public wants the annual federal budget to be balanced. On the other hand, hard times have not somehow magically been eliminated; the business cycle has not disappeared, and in time the economy again will turn down. Whether or not compensatory fiscal policy will regain the support it once had remains to be seen. 

As a nation we produce what we do essentially to feed, clothe and house ourselves. At the same time, we produce many services that complement and improve how well we carry out those basic functions. Simultaneously, too, we trade some of what we produce in goods and services with other countries for some of what they produce because we get more of goods and services from abroad for what we trade than what we would get were we to produce the same goods and services ourselves. 

One would think the advantages of trade being what they are, that countries would concentrate on producing those goods and services they can produce at lesser cost than other countries can produce in terms of the resources going into those goods and services. Generally, every country uses its resources in unique  ways, relative to the use of resources in other countries, to produce its output. The difference in the way countries employ their resources creates an advantage for each country in producing some goods and services in comparison with other countries, and puts them at a disadvantage in some other goods and services. Accordingly, countries have ample motivation to trade with one another; trade is clearly a means to enhance the consumption possibilities of the populations of countries. Yet, in spite of what can be gained from concentrating on goods and services in which they have comparative advantage and from engaging in open trade, industrialized countries to a substantial degree tend to produce the same goods and services produced by others, and to raise barriers in one form or another against the import of goods and services which compete with domestic production. 

Industrialized countries, and for that matter, lesser developed countries, tend to produce the same or at least similar goods and services because entrepreneurs whether in one country or another perceive profits to be made in producing their products. Once any product is made in a country, the firms producing it and their employees have a vested interest in curtailing import competition. Their interest is vested by the political influence they can exert in national government. Their demands for restrictions on imports competing with what they produce bring about barriers to trade. Jobs and return on capital of import competing industries in such countries are protected, but the economy of a country as a whole©©its standard of living is lower than it could be in more open trade. To be sure, increases in imports are bound to affect adversely some jobs and some invested capital. But trade is a two way street. Increased imports mean either increased exports or increases in foreign investment in the domestic economy. Increases in exports mean more jobs and more intensive use of capital in export industries. Of course, increases in foreign ownership of capital in the domestic economy by virtue of trade are associated with the export of profits on that capital. But the capital itself is an increase in domestic investment and added jobs that otherwise my not have taken place. 

Our economy is essentially a large firm economy. That is, only a relatively few large firms produce about half of our total output. Those firms produce goods and services not only for the domestic markets in the United States, but for markets abroad. As such, they seek to protect their domestic markets from import competition while advocating open trade abroad. Producers of goods and services in the rest of the industrialized world follow much the same pattern in their market strategies. 

Industrialized countries contrast sharply with the less developed countries of the world. In the former, the standard of living is high, the population is well educated, the population growth rate is slow, and typically but not always the political structure is stable. In the latter, the standard of living is low, literacy among the population is low, the birth rate is high, and in many of the less developed countries, political instability is common. The animus of modern world history can be found in the emergence of Western Europe from the Dark Ages in which over the succeeding centuries there were widespread increases in literacy, advances in agriculture, great scientific discoveries, dramatic adaptations in technology for producing output and simultaneously for waging war, followed by the conquest, subjegation and division into colonies of the rest of the world, and in the last couple of centuries the violent revolutions for independence from European powers by native populations in lands they had conquered. Out of all that emerged the United States following two world wars not only as an economy of unrivalled capability but a nation of unequalled military power. 

As an extension initially of Western European culture, traditions and technological development, the United States became what it is today from a fortuitous combination of expansion into geographical areas with bountiful natural resources, transformation into an industrial economy, wholesale influxes of European populations, and the development of mass consumer markets to absorb most of what its economy could produce. The mass markets in consumer products of the United States could hardly have developed from population and geographical growth alone. Of crucial importance to increases in its standard of living, the spectacular inventions and modern production methods between the end of the Civil War and the end of the nineteenth century propelled the economy into a new world of consumption and industrial power. The economies arising from large scale manufacturing advanced the productivity of labor in which productivity gains resulted in greater output per person employed. Economies of scale are traceable to the greater specialization of labor that large scale production permits, and the unique chemical, physical and thermal characteristics of plant and machinery that combine to produce proportionately more output for successively like increases in materials and labor. 

The pluralistic nature of American society, the organic and statutory character of our political structure, and the emergence of organized labor have played critical roles in the real income of working men and women. There is no agreement on the impact organized labor has had in the first half of this century on wages and salaries. Some say without organized labor, wages and salaries throughout the economy would have been lower than they turned out to be. Others say organized labor perversely held down wages and salaries in general. Whatever influence, good or bad, organized labor has had on wages and salaries, it is certain that had not income for working men and women kept pace with the value of what they were producing, long before the economy had become the industrialized power of this century, it would have stopped dead in its tracks. To be sure, labor conceivably might have been compensated far short of the value of its work while owners of capital traded national output with other economies for their own enrichment. But as it has turned out, the value of our national product has been distributed domestically as income in its various forms sufficient to absorb the total product. Again, it must be stressed that for an economy to prosper and grow over the long run, what is produced must be absorbed by consumption and capital investment. In the short run, the two, supply and demand can differ from one another precipitating or aggravating the business cycle, but in the long run, the two must be compatible with one another. And it is the prospect that a good or service can be sold at a profit that motivates an entrepreneur, whether already established or a new venturer, to translate new discoveries into marketable products. 

The modern economy is based on money as a medium of exchange to effect the distribution of income generated by production of goods and services. In the complexities of the modern economy, income is distributed as wages, salaries, interest, dividends and rent. Wages and salaries are compensation for employed labor, interest accrues to those who loan money to others, dividends are income payments out of profits paid to owners of corporate stock, and rent is income paid to owners of property let to others. 

In all industrialized countries including the United States the distribution of income is modified by government through taxes and transfer payments. Income of persons is taxed at different rates according to its level and form. Out of tax revenue and from what it borrows from time to time, government makes transfer payments to persons according to legally defined entitlements. Those payments commonly referred to as welfare payments are a redistribution of income by way of income taxes to remediate the perceived shortcomings of the industrialized economy in its ability by itself to effect an income distribution equitably for each person in its society to live well. In the absence of government intervention in the distribution of income, the structurally unemployed, the underemployed, the disabled, the aged, and the unskilled  would be shut out from sharing reasonably well in the national output. But the discrepancies in the distribution of income are as much a sociological problem as an economic problem in the industrialized country. Why persons are unemployed, why they are aged without adequate income, and certainly why they are disabled can be said to be questions for society to answer as opposed to be questions for the free economy to answer. In the United States, as in most of other industrialized countries, the society as an electorate of government has chosen to resolve the problem of inequities in income distribution through government intervention, and in doing so has framed the problem as an economic one. Alternatively, society might have taken on the resolution of the problem as a uniquely societal one resolved through charitable efforts and in which government was to play no part. But the problems of unemployment, underemployment, disability and aging while sociological in nature are not readily resolved in a large, pluralistic and industrialized society. In the United States, the gulf that exists between most minorities and the caucasian majority seemingly keeps those minorities at bay from developing economically enough to share more fully in the nation's output. 

However personal income is distributed, for an economy to become industrialized, some of that income must be set aside as savings to finance capital investment.  That is, an economy to industrialize must produce not only consumer goods and services but capital goods and services as well. The primitive economy the very underdeveloped economy©©consumes all it produces. Nothing is saved and nothing in the way of capital is produced. In the industrial economy, not only do persons save, but businesses as well save out of net profits©©that is businesses retain some of their profits for saving. The sum of personal and business saving is the total private saving of the domestic economy that is translated through a refined financial intermediation system into capital investment. In this role, the financial intermediation system acts as a middleman taking personal and business saving and passing it to businesses and persons as financing for investment. Our financial intermediation system is a complex and highly articulated network. Investment banks, commercial banks, credit unions, and savings and loan banks, bring savers and investors together in the financial markets of the economy. To get financing for planned investment, businesses issue securities as shares of stock or bonds or notes. Investment banks underwrite much of those issues of securities that is, they buy the securities©©and in turn offer them to the public at large at prices at which they can sell all they have bought at a profit. The public at large in this instance is made up principally of persons, mutual funds, pension funds, insurance firms and various organizations, including foreign interests. Individually, businesses may augment financing of planned investment in this way with their own saving, or they may use their savings to buy newly issued securities of other businesses. 

Coupled with the transactions in newly issued securities in financial markets, there is contiuous trading in secondary markets in already issued securities. Transactions in newly issued securities and trading in seconday markets determine prices of securities that provides a translation of saving into capital investment. The trading in already issued securities is REally an exchange of claims to investment already made, and does not represent new saving and investment. However, that trading in the secondary markets affects the prices of newly issued securities, and accordingly encourages or discourages business and industry in their planned capital investments. The stock and bond markets thus play a vital role in the growth rate of an economy. In a manner of speaking, the aspirations behind saving are a self©fullfilling prophesy; save a lot and the economy and its rewards may grow alot; save a little and its rewards are meager. 

In the industrial economy, not only is there private investment to be financed, but there is public investment as well that must be financed if we are to have schools, highways, water and sewer systems and other public services. In the United States, the translation of saving into public capital investment is effected by the marketing of newly issued income securities much in the same way that saving is turned into private investment. However, not all securities issued by the federal government and states and local jurisdictions are for financing public investment. U.S. Treasury original issue securities finance current budget deficits created by a shortfall of general revenue relative to total outlays.  U.S. Treasury securities are issued in the form of 90 day and 180 day bills, two and ten year notes, and bonds with 30 year and other lengthy maturities. Over the last seven decades with rare exception annual budget deficits have piled up one after the other requiring the Treasury more or less continuously to refinance maturing bills, notes and bonds while adding to the total by borrowing more. 

The outlays that the U.S. Treasury finances by issuing securities may or may not be classed as public investment. That part of total outlays exceeding total revenue of the federal government that are of an investment nature are in competition with private investment for financing by domestic saving and the foreign trade deficit. Budgetary deficit financing by the Treasury, whatever it purpose may be, adds to the overall demand for borrowed money, and thus causes interest rates for all ranges of maturities to be higher than they otherwise would be. Higher interest rates eliminate some private investment that would have been made, a reflection that through the political process, the public has decided that public investment, whatever it might be, is more valuable than the private investment it has displaced. Be that investment defense capability, environmental protection or interstate highway expansion, in the eyes of the public its incremental value relative to its cost exceeds what could be realized by the economy as a whole through displaced private investmewnt. 

That part of the excess of outlays over revenues in the federal government's annual budget that essentially is payments for consumption purposes, that is, for example, entitlement payments or subsidies for housing, food, medical care and agricultural crops, also adversely affects private investment while not increasing public investment. Those outlays, of course, could be financed by increased income taxes, but the taxpaying public while acknowledging the need for income distribution balks at higher taxes to pay for it. In the end, however, entitlement payments and other such outlays by government, redistribute claims to what the economy has produced, and in doing so causes some of total production to shift from capital goods and services to consumption goods and services. 

Deficit financing of welfare payments by government in the past has been precipitated by the public's unwillingness to pay for them with income tax increases. Were the public willing to pay for welfare with higher taxes, any current federal deficit would reflect more closely outlays for public investment purposes, and changes in public investment would be keyed to compensatory fiscal policy changes. Therein lies a dilemma of sorts  for compensatory policy. Surely, the need for public investment does not appear only in times of high unemployment and low output. Does it make economic sense, then, to make that investment only in recessionary or depressed times not to achieve the purposes of the investment itself but primarily to increase overall demand for output. 

This discussion of the circular flow of the economy has attempted to show that the main elements of that flow consumer demand, output of consumer goods and services, savings, investment, technology advances, international trade, demand for public goods and services, public investment, monetary policy and compensatory fiscal policy can not be brought into synchronization such that the economy will balance itself at full employment with stable prices while growing in its capacity to produce at a steady rate. 

As we will see in the next chapter, consumer demand is made up of the demand for a myriad of goods and services, always undergoing change by its whimsical nature, improvements in the standard of living, the changing panoply of new products and changes among relative prices of all goods and services, in addition to adjustments made from time to time in allocation of current income between personal saving and current consumption. 

Producers of consumer goods and services base their output schedules on past experience and how they believe consumption patterns are likely to change. As they discern changes that are taking place, they may be scrambling to increase output of some goods and services while desperately cutting back on the output of others, never quite being able to mesh the rate at what they produce with the rate at which consumption is taking place. The question of how much to produce for the individual producer is confounded by the competition among all producers for consumption spending. 

The allocation of current income by persons into expenditures for current consumption and savings affects the cost of financing private investment as well as public investment. The greater the rate of personal savings and, too, corporate savings, the lower the cost of financing is for investment, other things being what they are. One would have to say the greater the rate of saving, the greater might be the rate of economic growth precipitated by the investment encouraged by lower financing costs. 

The amount of investment taking place in an economy depends, too, on the transformation of new discoveries and ideas into expanded capability to produce outplut. New discoveries coming out of laboratories and elsewhere are always being made, some of which can be exploited for commercial purposes. But the rate of investment coming from new discoveries and ideas does not follow a predictable pattern. Once in a while something like electricity, the railroad, the auto, the radio or the computer is discovered or invented and exploited with startling results; at other times advances in technology may be quite prosaic. Whatever may be taking place, one can be sure that saving and investment are never quite in tune with one another. And their nature is such that in the short run, investment depends partially on saving while in the long run saving depends partially on investment. 

The trade that an economy carries on with the rest of the world affects the level of domestic output in three ways. With trade there is likely a greater overall demand for domestic goods and services. At the same time, while exports account for an increase in output of some goods and services, imports in competing with some domestically produced goods and services, cause a reduction of their output below what it otherwise would be. That reduction, however, is moderated by the increased purchasing power of the economy as a whole stemming from distribution of income generated by exports. 

Finally, an economy trading merchandise with the rest of the world typically runs a deficit or surplus. Rarely and not for long will its current account, as that trade is called, be in balance. The difference as a deficit represents the extent of investment the rest of the world is making in the domestic economy net of what the domestic economy is making abroad. That difference as a surplus represents the extent of investment the dometic economy is making in the rest of the world net of what the rest of the world is making in the domestic economy. Investment as used in this context is somewhat misleading since a deficit or surplus is a deficiency or an excess of payments to foreigners for their goods and services relative to payments by foreigners for domestic output. Thus, a deficit means that foreigners have accumulated claims against the domestic currency. Those claims in reality become an additional source of financing for domestic investment©©private investment as well as public investment©©and perhaps the current budgetary deficit of the government. A trade deficit represents in one way or another increased foreign ownership of the capital base of the domestic economy, and to the degree it has taken place, increased foreign credit extended to domestic government. 

Does foreign ownership portend foreign control of the domestic economy and possibly of its government? For a small country, trading a significant share of its output to the rest of the world for foreign investment, the danger of control by outsidersa is real. Foreign ownership of domestic resources relative to domestic ownership can be quite large. The United States and Panama are good examples of the relative degree of foreign ownership. Foreign ownership in the United States is roughly ten percent of its industrial capital base, and is hardly a consideration economically or politically in domestic matters. On the other hand, in Panama, past United States ownership of the Panama Canal and other assets in Panama has meant defacto control of that country's political and economic life. 

For the country able to maintain its economic and political sovereignty©©and large industrialized countries are able to do so©©a trade deficit may mean not only an additional source of financing for domestic investment, but investment in improved productivity that otherwise might not have taken place. 

Many are those who decry the accumulated trade deficits of the United States, but without those deficits the domestic capital base very probably would have been less than it is. Foreign ownership of our capital base is greater and the return on that capital must be paid to its owners in one way or another, but the bulk of income generated by that capital is paid to domestic labor. One might think of a trade deficit as a translation of payments to foreigners for imports into something that augments domestic saving. 

What goods and services should the government provide? How much and what kind of capital investment should government make? The public's answers to those questions change over time. Today, the public seems to want less government involvement in output of goods and services than in time past. In the United States, each of the three levels of government, federal, state and local,  has been assigned unique responsibilities by the U.S. Constitution and the statutory framework built up on its foundation. At the same time, the three levels work in conjunction with one another in providing some public goods and services. A good or service becomes a public one by virtue of a decision by the public through the political process that government should be primarily responsible for providing it to them. But the public does change its mind from time to time as to what constitutes a public good or service, and currently some what once were considered sacrosanct responsibilities of government are being reinterpreted because  of dissatisfaction of the public with government performance and the public's belief that private enterprise can provide what government now does at a lesser cost. In education, water and sewer sytems, highways, utilities, police protection, package and letter delivery, and economic welfare, government at all three levels has ceded more and more of its hold on those areas to private enterprise. 

At the same time, the electorate has grown weary and impatient with the public welfare system put in place by its very own insistence some thirty five years ago, and seems to be now insisting on substantial cutbacks in outlays for food, housing, medical care and general assistance for the lpoor. Only in national defense does the federal government continue its monopoly. The public's demand for national defense varies according to circumstances. Changes in national defense outlays and public welfare payments differ from changes in other categories of government spending in that changes in outlays for those two categories do not precipitate compensatory changes in the demand for private goods and services as readily as do changes in outlays for other public goods and services. Certainly a cutback in defense spending does not trigger or cause a shift to like spending by the public in an elevated demand from private suppliers. National defense in a democracy is indeed the unique responsibility of the central government. 

On the other hand, public welfare is not a unique responsibility of government. From the beginning of our republic, in keeping with European traditions, public charities alone took care of the poor until it became more and more evident to the electorate that in the society at large, charitable organizations by themselves were inadequate to the task of providing for the poor. 

The amount of money in circulation throughout the economy and associated interest rate play a crucial role in the flow of activity in the economy related to the output of goods and services, their prices, and the level of employment. As we have seen, the control of the money supply and interest rates is exerted through monetary policy.  As earlier referred to, that policy is the responsibility of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, the central bank of the United States. The Federal Reserve System, which is comprised of twelve geographical districts, was created in 1913 by federal law. Since its inception, various changes have been made in its functions and structure which have added to what it  can do in monetary matters. The Board of Governors is made up of seven members each appointed, at staggered intervals, for seven years by the President. At its head is the Chairman, one of the seven members, but serving in that capacity for a four year stint by Presidential appointmenmt. The President may reappoint the Chairman to additional tenures. The authority of the FRS in monetary matters is extensive, but the Board of Governors invokes monetary policy primarily through its Federal Open Market Account, an activity centered in the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. The FOMA trades in outstanding issues of U.S. Trasury securities, adding to its inventory by purchases, and once in awhile reducing its holdings by letting some of them run to maturity and not replacing them, or by sales in the open market. The manager of the FOMA gets instructions from the Federal Open Market Committee, made up of the seven governors and representatives of selected Federal Reserve Districts. In purchasing Treasury bills, note, or bonds, the FOMA, representing the twelve Districts, pays for what it buys with a check drawn against the Districts or freshly minted Federal Reserve Notes. That check effectively turns U.S. Treasury securities into money the transaction is referred to as monetizing the federal debt and increases the monetary base underlying our money supply. The seller of a U.S. Treasury security can be an individual, a commercial bank, a nonbank corporation, or a foreigner©©anyone who might have bought the security from the Treasury when originally issued. Typically, the seller, if not a commercial bank will deposit the FOMA check in a commercial bank and may set the stage for a multiple expansion of that check into an increase in the money supply which is made up of cash in the hands of the public and the total of various checkbook accounts. The cash we have is the sum of Federal Reserve Notes in the various denominations in which they are printed, and the coins minted by the U.S. Treasury. 

Our commercial banking system is required by law to maintain certain reserves against its deposits. Those reserves must be cash in its vaults or deposits in the Federal Reserve System. But the commercial banking system in the United States operates on a fractional reserve basis. Thus, a bank taking in a Federal Reserve check as a new deposit need set aside only a fraction of it to satisfy its legal reserve requirement, and then is free to put the rest of it to work as a loan in one form or another. In making a loan, the bank begins a chain reaction, so to speak, as that loan either in cash or an additional deposit against itself finds its way first in another bank and then another, being reduced at each turn by each bank's legal requirement, but each time adding to the money supply. In this way, "high powered" money the FOMA injects into the banking system by its purchases of Treasury securities is expanded into a multiple of itself, and that multiple is an addition to the money supply. The increase in the money supply represents a pickup in activity in terms of the purposes for which the new bank money is used. People and firms borrow money to demand goods and services from others. The banks in the system expand the money supply by making loans simply because they are profit making corporations and finding themselves with fresh legal reserves use them to best advantage. 

Banks make those loans to individuals and firms. Individuals for the most part borrow money for consumption purposes or to buy a place to live. Firms borrow money to finance an expansion of output, or to improve the way they produce their output. When banks expand their loans in the manner just described, they typically do so at lower interest rates, thus enticing consumers and producing firms to borrow what they would not at higher rates.Consumers set the incremental cost of each dollar they borrow against the increased sense of satisfaction they believe they will experience. Firms set the incremental cost of each dollar they borrow against the incremental increase in profit they anticipate. 

Monetary policy works in one direction or the other. When times are bad, increases in high powered money can help in starting the economy on the road to recovery. When the economy is overheated in terms of demand for goods and services exceeding supply and causing prices to rise, the FOMA can siphon out high powered money from the banking system by reducing some of its holdings of U.S. Treasury securities. The effect of reducing its holdings is to reduce the money supply causing interest rates to rise and loan demand to fall off. Thus, changes in monetary policy can affect conditions of supply and demand for produced goods and services and the level of employment. 

We have seen how fiscal policy changes by way of changes in demand by government for goods and services from the private economy and by way of changes in taxes and transfer payments cause changes in overall demand for goods and services, and accordingly in time changes in the economy's total output and the level of employment. 

Fiscal policy changes and monetary policy changes in theory when coordinated and effected in small incremental steps could compensate for departures taken in the supply of and the demand for goods and services tending to knock the economy off a full employment growth path at stable prices. But monetary policy and fiscal policy are each in the hands of separate policy makers somewhat independent of one another. Monetary policy is the province of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors, while fiscal policy is made by the President and Congress. Although the ultimate objective of the two in implementing policy is a high employment economy growing at stable prices, Congress and the President are elected to their positions by the people, and lean towards policy changes producing a more visible and immediate effect on after tax income and employment, while the Board of Governors being appointed and independent of the electorate historically has been more concerned with maintaining stable prices. The two groups, because of the difference in focus are not infrequently at odds with one another. 

We live by what we produce. How well we live depends primarily on our own talents, but we can see that how well we live depends, too, on government economic and social policy, on the business cycle and on the progress of technology. Individually, our talents and good fortune may carry us beyond the setbacks inflicted from time to time by bad policy, business cycle slumps and stagnent technoloy; or talents and good fortune may be reinforced by good policy, business cycle upswings and advances in technology. We never know exactly what is in store for us.
