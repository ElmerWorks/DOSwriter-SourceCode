LIFETIME FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

I. The Economy In Which We Live And Our Daily Bread

1. The Industrial Economy

As a family or as a single person, we are members of a national
economy and society making our contributions to its performance,
and being rewarded for our efforts according to the value the
economy and society determine for them. The reward we get for our
efforts as income is what we use to pay for our current needs and
to provide for the future. As a family or as a single person, we
live our lives as independent people, but the twists and turns
the economy and society as a whole take over time work their
influence on what fate has in store for us. The better we
understand how the economy works and its interaction with our
society, the better we will be at managing for today and planning
for tomorrow. So let us look into the nature and motivation of
the modern economy.

In a society we live by what we produce. And in a world made up
of national economies, we trade some of what we produce with
other countries for what they produce. What we produce and what
we get in trade with other countries is based on the demand we
place on ourselves for privately produced goods and services as
well as those goods and services we insist be produced by
government. Individually, we exert a demand for some of those
goods and services according to our unique preferences and the
income we have to satisfy those preferences.

A modern economy is based on money as a means for exchanging
goods and services. There is a circular flow of activity in the
production of goods and services in which workers of all kinds
are compensated individually with money for what they do,
representing their share of those goods and services available in
the marketplaces of the economy. For any one individual, however,
that income may be not only be in the form of wages and salary,
but also in the form of dividends from ownership in producing
firms, interest from loans made to others, payments by
government, rent from property, and gifts from others.

Wages or salary as a share of goods and services depend on the
value of a person's labor in producing those goods and services;
what that value might be is determined not only by the
availability of labor skills, but by the demand of the public at
large for the goods and services produced by those skills. The
compensation of a highly talented professional athlete, a
construction worker, a medical specialist, an office worker, a
movie actor, and a computer programmer depends on their
availability and the value the public at large puts on what they
produce. For most walks of life, education plays an important
role in the income paid to individuals for the work they do. So
much of what the public wants today requires skills developed
only through higher education.

The need for those skills is traceable to successive scientific
discoveries which have been exploited through investment in the
means to produce goods and services that enhance our standard of
living. The greater skills required to produce more valuable
output are reflected in higher real wages and salaries which are
transformed into a demand for the very goods and services they
are instrumental in producing. So we can see if an economy is to
enjoy increases in its standard of living there must be
investment in new products and better ways to produce all
products. Ther must also be increases in wages and salaries
reflective of greater output, and a transformation of that income
into demand for the greater output.

Investment in new plant, machinery and new production techniques
takes place where there is a promise of profit from the marketing
of goods and services produced by the investment. Investment in
productive capital in an advanced economy is made by
entrepreneurs large and small. A large industrial firm like
General Motors invests in improved means to produce autos it
already produces, while one or a few individuals on a very small
scale may create an entirely new product such as as personal
computer by the investment of their time and meager resources. In
both instances, the motivation is expectation of profit. Both
ventures may prove to be successful. In the one, a large company
replaces existing plant and equipment with more efficient capital
to produce the same product. In the other, daring entrepreneurs
start an entirely new industry to produce a product new to the
consuming public, but a product they believe will be in demand
for the efficiences and conveniences it opens up for its users.

Whatever form it takes, investment in capital in a modern economy
expands the capabilities of labor while men and women, to keep up
with advancing vintages of capital must successively respond to
the requirements for new and improved skills technology imposes
on them.

Capital and labor are the key ingredients for producing goods and
services. But natural resources are an equally important
ingredient. An industrial economy could not exist in the absence
of access to natural resources. Some economies such as the United
States are favored by providence and good fortune with an
abundance of productive agricultural land, minerals, fossil
fuels, water and a relatively benign climate. Other economies
such as Japan may not have all they need as industrial powers
within their borders but have access to them through trade with
other countries.

The world today is divided into industrial economies with
advanced standards of living and economies in various stages of
development with much lower standards of living. Why there are
such great disparities in industrial capabilities is a question
that has no ready answer. But one can be sure that the persistent
buildup of capital, continuing improvement in labor skills and
assured access in one way or another to natural resources are
indispensable to industrial capabilities. The underpinning of it
all seems to be an enlightened and educated population from which
have come the scientific discoveries on which industrial
processes are based; a population which has fomented the spirit
of entrepreneurship; and a population which has provided not only
the labor skills compatible with technological applications of
scientific discoveries, but the demand for the goods and services
producible by industrialization. The industrial age in which we
live began in Great Britain some 250 years ago. By that time
Great Britain and Western Europe were already technologically far
ahead of the rest of the world. Along with what would become the
United States and later Japan, their further development rested
on the characteristics of their populations as described above.

Were it somehow possible for an economy to maintain demand for
output in keeping with its supply as improvements in goods and
services were made, the economy could grow steadily at rates
governed by variations in discoveries and the investment
necessary to take advantage of them. As it is, demand for output
and the supply of output only intermittently and temporarily
coincide with one another. At any one time each is either rising
or falling for its own reasons. Changes in output and supply that
differ from one another underlie the undulations of the business
cycle, a phenomenon of the industrial economy in which producers
of output and consumers of output are generally  free to follow
their own objectives. Generally, producers seek to maximize
profits from marketing the goods and services they produce.
Consumers seek to maximize their level of satisfaction from
consuming goods and services arrayed before them in the
marketplaces. By and large, the array of goods and services
available to consumers depends in part on the capacity to produce
them that prior investment makes possible. What prior investment
has been made was motivated, as observed above, by expectations
of profit in satisfying anticipated demand for products that
investment has made possible. Whatever form investment takes,
whether to produce a new product, reduce the cost of producing an
existing product or expand the capacity to produce more of the
same product, there is always some uncertainty for the individual
producer about how much of what is produced will be absorbed by
demand. At a time of high output and employment, demand for what
has been produced is strong, and producers tend to think demand
will continue and even grow stronger and that production can
expand to satisfy that rising demand with little impact on
average cost to produce. Their optimism may be borne out for a
while but soomer or later they will be disappointed for several
reasons. In an industrial economy, any one good or service may be
produced by a number of firms in competition with one another.
Each of  the firms typically will sense the same opportunity, and
together in producing more in time almost certainly will produce
more than the market, that is, demand, is willing to absorb at
current prices, dampening their enthusiasm for maintaining output
at current levels. At the same time, the increase in employment
made by all producers in raising production will be made
typically only at higher wages and salaries, further reducing the
difference between revenue and cost.
